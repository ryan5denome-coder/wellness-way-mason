When someone is exhausted, has dark circles, and bruises easily, one of the first places we want to look is iron: not only whether they are anemic today, but whether their iron stores are in a good place.

A lot of people are told their iron looks "normal" after a basic blood count, and they leave still tired. Some have already been taking iron and still don't feel like themselves. The Wellness Way has been writing about that exact frustration for years. In [Why Aren't My Iron Supplements Fixing My Anemia?](https://www.thewellnessway.com/anemia/), they make two points we use in clinic all the time: you can have plenty of iron and still be anemic, and you can be iron deficient and not be anemic.

A lab range is a range, not a goal, and two different iron tests can answer two different questions. This post is not a diagnosis, and it is not advice to start or stop any supplement or medication. Talk with your own doctor before you change anything.

## Hemoglobin and ferritin answer different questions

Hemoglobin is part of a complete blood count. It tells you something about your red blood cells right now, including whether you meet the usual definition of anemia.

Ferritin tells a different story. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes ferritin as a good marker of iron stores, and recommends using ferritin to assess iron status in individuals ([WHO, Use of ferritin concentrations to assess iron status](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/ferritin-concentrations)). You can think of hemoglobin as what's in the checkout line, and ferritin as what's left on the shelves in the back. The front of the store can still look open while the stockroom is getting low.

That's why a hemoglobin in range does not automatically mean your iron stores have been checked. If you only had a finger-stick or a basic blood count, ferritin may never have been ordered.

## It's not always just a matter of taking iron

The Wellness Way's article [Anemia: What's The Real Cause?](https://www.thewellnessway.com/anemia-whats-the-real-cause/) walks through why a low-iron story is incomplete if you stop at the supplement aisle. Anemia can be related to blood loss, to whether the body is making or breaking down red blood cells, and to other nutrients besides iron, including vitamin B12 and folate. They also describe anemia of inflammation, sometimes called anemia of chronic disease, in which iron can look different in the blood than it does in the tissues because the body is dealing with ongoing inflammation.

That last piece matters for lab reading. Ferritin can also read higher when there is inflammation, infection, or certain other conditions, so a "normal" ferritin does not always rule out a store problem. WHO notes that inflammation should be taken into account when ferritin is used ([WHO, ferritin concentrations](https://www.who.int/tools/elena/interventions/ferritin-concentrations)). In practice, ferritin is best read alongside other markers, not as a single checkbox.

The Wellness Way also looks at anemia through the 3 Ts: Traumas (including physical stress and blood loss, such as a very heavy period), Toxins (including inflammation, infections, and other biochemical stressors), and Thoughts (the mental stress that can wear on the whole system). We don't use this post to name a single "optimal" number for everyone. The useful move is to ask for the actual number, look at it next to how you feel, and look at the rest of the panel with a practitioner who can put it in context.

## Symptoms people notice

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) lists symptoms of iron-deficiency anemia that include fatigue and weakness, pale skin, shortness of breath, dizziness, cold hands and feet, brittle nails, and unusual cravings such as ice, among others ([NHLBI, Iron-Deficiency Anemia](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/anemia/iron-deficiency-anemia)). The Wellness Way's anemia article adds foggy thinking, a racing or irregular heartbeat, and headache as symptoms people often report.

Those symptoms have more than one possible explanation, and they are not a diagnosis of low iron on their own. They are a reason to look, especially if they have been hanging around, if your periods are heavy, if you've been told you look "fine" on a basic blood count, or if iron alone hasn't changed how you feel.

Heavy periods matter here. If you are soaking through pads, bleeding more than 7 days, or wiping out for a week after every cycle, iron stores belong in the conversation. We wrote more about when period symptoms are worth a closer look in our post on [period pain in Mason, OH](/blog/period-pain-mason-oh).

## What labs to ask for

You can take this list to your doctor or bring it to a visit with us. Your practitioner can tell you which of these already make sense in your situation. The Wellness Way's anemia article lists a complete blood count, iron studies, vitamin B12, folate, and thyroid testing among the labs often used to sort this out, and sometimes a stool test if hidden blood loss is a concern.

- **Ferritin**, to look at iron stores
- **A complete blood count (CBC)**, including hemoglobin
- **Serum iron**
- **Iron saturation**, also called transferrin saturation
- **Total iron-binding capacity (TIBC)**, when your practitioner wants a fuller iron panel
- **Vitamin B12 and folate**, because iron is not the only nutrient involved in healthy red blood cells
- **A marker of inflammation**, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), when ferritin needs context

Ask for the number, not just "it's fine." If someone has already run "iron," it's still worth knowing whether that meant a hemoglobin, a ferritin, or a full panel, and how recently it was done.

If you donate blood often, it's also fair to ask whether ferritin has ever been checked. A finger-stick at a donation center is usually about hemoglobin that day, which is a different question than your stores over time. That's a conversation with your doctor, not advice to stop donating.
