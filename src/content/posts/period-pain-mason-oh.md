Period pain is not a rite of passage. It's not "just part of being a woman," and it's not something you have to quietly organize your month around.

If you've ever cancelled plans, left work early, or spent a day on the bathroom floor and been told that's normal, this is for you. At The Wellness Way Mason, we hear that story a lot. The Wellness Way has a simple way of saying it: PMS and painful periods may be common, but that does not make them a sign of good health. In [Tips for Easing a Painful Period](https://www.thewellnessway.com/periods-heavy-irregular-and-painful/), The Wellness Way writes that heavier flow, irregular cycles, or more painful cramps are "not normal, however common it may be." Their article [PMS: It May Be Common, But It's Not Normal](https://www.thewellnessway.com/pms-symptoms/) makes the same point for the week before your period.

This post is a plain-language look at what's worth a closer look, and how hormones, blood sugar, iron, gut health, and stress can play in. It is not a diagnosis, and it does not replace a visit with your own doctor or gynecologist.

## Your cycle is bigger than a few hard days

The Wellness Way reminds us that a period is only the visible part of a hormonal cycle that runs all month. We often talk about the body as a Swiss watch: when one part is under stress, other parts can start to feel it too. That is why we don't look at cramps as a problem to mask and move on from. We want to know what is stressing the body.

The Wellness Way groups those stressors into three categories, often called the 3 Ts: Traumas (physical stress), Toxins (chemical stress, including food and environmental exposures), and Thoughts (mental and emotional stress). Their PMS article frames many premenstrual symptoms in that same light, including a hormone pattern they discuss often, sometimes called estrogen dominance, which can come from a mix of those stressors.

## What is worth a closer look

These are patterns worth bringing to your gynecologist, and that, in our office, make us want a fuller picture.

- Pain that regularly keeps you from work, school, or plans
- Cramps so bad you feel sick, dizzy, or wiped out
- Pain that starts days before bleeding, or that doesn't ease after the first couple of days
- Pain that used to be manageable and has gotten worse over time
- A flow that lasts more than 7 days, soaks through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours, or wakes you up at night to change
- A cycle that is much shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or a period that disappears for months
- Mood changes in the week before your period that feel like a different version of you

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) notes that if you are soaking through a pad or tampon every hour for more than 2 hours and you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, or feel lightheaded or dizzy, you should seek emergency care right away ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/abnormal-uterine-bleeding)). A family pattern of brutal cramps is worth asking about, not just accepting.

## Hormones, blood sugar, iron, gut health, and stress

**Hormones.** The second half of the cycle, after ovulation, is when a lot of people notice the short fuse, the heavy fatigue, or the "be extra gentle" week. The Wellness Way's article [How Do You Know if Your Hormones Are Messed Up?](https://www.thewellnessway.com/how-do-you-know-if-your-hormones-are-messed-up/) explains that estrogen and progesterone need to work together, and that issues can show up in how hormones are made, converted, or cleared. Cycle-timed hormone labs, drawn on the right day, can show part of that picture. A random Tuesday blood draw often cannot.

**Blood sugar.** Big swings in blood sugar can leave you shaky, hungry, tired, or more irritable, and those swings are easier to feel in the week before a period. The Wellness Way often points to sugar as a stressor that can make hormone symptoms louder. Regular meals and fewer long gaps without food are a simple place to start. If your cycles are also very far apart or hard to predict, blood sugar and ovulation are worth looking at together. That's one reason we have a local page on [PCOS care in Mason, OH](/pcos-mason-oh).

**Iron.** A heavy flow can leave your iron stores low, and low iron can add to the wiped-out feeling that some people blame only on their period. Ferritin, which reflects iron stores, is a different question than a basic hemoglobin check.

**Gut health and toxins.** The Wellness Way's period-pain article looks at gut health, food, and everyday exposures as part of the period conversation, not as a side note. We don't guess which foods or exposures matter for you. We test when it's a fit.

**Thoughts and physical stress.** A packed month and poor sleep can make an already hard cycle feel harder. Stress is not the whole story, and it is not a reason to be told "just relax." It is one of the 3 Ts, and it belongs in the picture.

## Practical things to try

None of these replaces a visit if your pain is severe, or testing if your cycle has been hard for a long time. They are simple supports.

**Track the month.** Write down when bleeding starts, how heavy it is, when the pain starts, and when your mood or energy shifts. Several cycles of detail help us, and your gynecologist, see the pattern.

**Use heat, movement, and sleep.** The Wellness Way's period-pain article suggests a heating pad, hot water bottle, or warm bath for cramps, plus light movement like a walk or stretching, and enough sleep. A slightly earlier bedtime the night before day one is a reasonable place to start.

**Keep meals steadier, and go easier on sugar.** This is not a strict diet. It's about not arriving at day 21 already running on coffee and sweets. If prayer is part of your life, this is a natural place for it.

If you and your gynecologist are already talking about medical options, that conversation belongs with them. We don't use this blog to give medication advice. In The Wellness Way's language, we would rather test and then rebuild than only put out the symptom.

If your calendar already plans around your cycle, that's information. Lab testing is how we look at the pattern: cycle-timed hormones, iron stores, blood sugar over time, and, when it's a fit, gut health or food reactions. A "normal" checkbox is not always the end of the conversation.
