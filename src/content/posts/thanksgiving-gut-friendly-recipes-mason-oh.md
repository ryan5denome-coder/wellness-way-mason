Thanksgiving is a wonderful day for gathering, and it's also a lot of food, a lot of sitting, and often a lot of stress in a short window. Richer dishes, second helpings, less sleep, and more sugar than usual can leave your gut feeling noisy the next morning.

This isn't a post about skipping the meal you look forward to all year, or about making a speech at someone else's table. At The Wellness Way, the holiday advice is more practical than that: lean on real, whole foods, keep the plate simpler where you can, and don't turn dinner into a debate. In a November 2024 student training recap, [Staying Healthy Through the Holidays](https://www.thewellnessway.com/student-club-training-recap-from-november-13-2024-staying-healthy-through-the-holidays/), Dr. Shane Husyer, D.C., summed it up as real whole food, plenty of vegetables, some fruit, quality protein, and less sugar, gluten, dairy, and fast food, while meeting people where they are. A December 2023 recap, [Handling Holiday Stress & Foods](https://www.thewellnessway.com/student-club-training-recap-from-december-20-2023-handling-holiday-stress-foods/), made the same point even more simply: healthy meats, prepped vegetables, and fruit are a solid foundation.

The two recipes below, rosemary roasted root vegetables and cinnamon baked apples, are the ones we shared in our November 18 Wellness Way Wednesday newsletter. They feel special, they don't ask a lot of you, and they bring color and fiber to a table that's often heavy on the richer stuff.

## Why Thanksgiving can feel like a lot for your gut

A holiday plate is usually a mix of richer foods your body doesn't see together every week, eaten a little faster, then followed by a long stretch of sitting and conversation. Add travel, later nights, and the mental load of a full house, and your digestion has more to adapt to than just the food.

The Wellness Way looks at that through the 3 Ts: Traumas (physical stress, including lack of sleep and too much sitting), Toxins (including sugar, processed foods, and foods you don't tolerate well), and Thoughts (the emotional stress of the season). Food is one part of the picture. Stress is another. Neither one makes you a failure.

You can still enjoy the day and make a few choices that are kinder to your gut. The Wellness Way's holiday trainings also suggest not making a big production of your plate. A simple "no, thank you," or bringing one dish you feel good about, is enough.

## Keep one dish simple, and keep it real

You don't have to redesign someone else's menu. If you're bringing a side, make it the simple one. If you're hosting, let one dish be a sheet pan that mostly takes care of itself.

A tray of roasted vegetables or a pan of baked apples can still feel like a feast. It also gives you something familiar and warm on the table, made from whole ingredients. People who want a lighter plate can serve themselves more of that dish without making a speech about it.

The Wellness Way also notes that a gluten-free label is not automatically a healthier choice if the food is still highly processed. Their article [Is a Gluten-free Diet Healthy?](https://www.thewellnessway.com/is-a-gluten-free-diet-healthy/) points out that swapping one packaged product for another doesn't automatically support the gut. Whole-food sides, like the recipes below, are an easier way to add something simple to the table.

## Rosemary roasted root vegetables

This is a colorful, filling side that brings fiber to the plate and sits nicely next to richer dishes.

**You will need**

- 2 carrots, chopped
- 2 parsnips, chopped
- 1 sweet potato, chopped
- 1 red onion, chopped
- 2 tablespoons olive oil or melted grass-fed butter
- Sea salt and pepper
- 1 tablespoon fresh rosemary

Cut the vegetables into similar-sized pieces so they cook evenly. Toss everything together on a sheet pan. Roast at 425 degrees Fahrenheit for 30 to 35 minutes, flipping halfway, until the edges are golden and the centers are tender.

That's the whole method. If you want a head start, chop the vegetables in the morning and keep them in the fridge until it's time to roast. Dried rosemary works too; use about a teaspoon.

## Cinnamon baked apples

This is a warm dessert that makes the house smell like the holidays and doesn't ask you to roll out a crust. It's sweet enough to feel special, and simple enough to bake while the main dish is resting.

**You will need**

- 4 apples, cored and halved
- 2 tablespoons melted butter or coconut oil
- 1 tablespoon honey or maple syrup
- 1 teaspoon cinnamon
- A pinch of sea salt
- Optional: a handful of chopped walnuts

Heat the oven to 375 degrees Fahrenheit. Place the apples cut-side up in a baking dish, drizzle with the butter and honey, and sprinkle with cinnamon and salt. Bake 25 to 30 minutes, until tender. Add the walnuts in the last 5 minutes if you like them toasted.

Any firm baking apple will work. If you're cooking for a mix of tastes, set the walnuts on the side.

## A few small habits for the day of

**Walk a little after you eat.** A short, easy walk after dinner, even a loop around the block before dessert, can be a kind reset. If it's cold or rainy, a few laps around the house still count. A [2022 systematic review in the journal Sports Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01649-4) found that short bouts of light walking, often just 2 to 5 minutes, were linked with a smaller rise in blood sugar after eating, compared with staying seated.

**Slow the first few bites.** The room will be busy, and you don't have to eat in silence. Just give the first part of the meal a little more time, and notice when you're comfortably satisfied.

**Keep water nearby, and leave room for what you love.** It's easy to forget water when other drinks are being poured all afternoon. If pie is the thing you wait for all year, enjoy a slice. The Wellness Way's holiday advice is about better choices, not perfection.
